Selected work

Three systems, and the evals that judged them.

Each one is a full build: a typed object model, an agent that can only reach data through it, and a measurement designed before the result was known. Two of the three returned findings that contradicted the premise I started with. Those are published as the headline rather than buried.

01

triage

Escalation-calibrated support agent
LiveEval runPython

An agent that reads a consumer financial complaint the moment it arrives and decides one thing: can this be closed with an explanation, or does it need a person with the authority to grant relief?

Built on 396,952 real complaints from the CFPB Consumer Complaint Database, 2021 to 2025. The deliverable is not the agent, it is a curve: for every error rate a support lead might accept, how much of the queue closes unattended. That turns "is the agent good" into "at what error budget, and would you sign it."

The inconvenient result

The eval ran on 500 held-out complaints at $50.44 of model spend, and it came back unflattering. The stated confidence is not calibrated: expected calibration error 0.22 against the 0.02 the weak baselines get for free, and no operating point in the tested range clears a 10% false-resolution rate. It is published as the eval rather than routed around.

A premise test ran before the agent was built, on the theory that a product with a false premise should be found out cheaply. It showed the narrative is worth less than the structured dropdowns, and that the single most predictive fact about a complaint is which company it was filed against. One respondent granted relief on 0.00% of 18,216 complaints; another on 42% of 22,975. So the agent runs company-blind by default, and the cost of that choice is measured rather than assumed.

02

probe

Adversarial search against tool-using agents
LiveHeld-outPython

An automated search that proposes structured attacks against a tool-using agent, breeds the ones that score, and grades every episode mechanically against a clean control run of the same case.

Then it hardens the target, runs a second search it has never seen, and reports the difference. That second number is the point, because hardening against a list of attacks you already found is easy and mostly worthless. Every target is a system I built and host. There is no LLM anywhere in the grading path, because a judge is attackable by the thing it grades.

The finding

Four defence layers cut attack success from 100% to 26%. Splitting the same episodes by delivery channel rather than attack class explains the rest: the stack closes both data channels completely and barely touches the user turn. That is not four layers underperforming, it is four layers doing exactly what they were built for. All of them defend the boundary between data the agent read and instructions it follows. None governs what the principal is allowed to ask for.

A held-out search on a budget ledger locked until the final milestone, run against the already-hardened target, recovered the undefended rate outright: a +74 point generalisation gap. Every mechanism it found walked straight to the one door the stack does not cover. A large gap that is fully explainable is a better outcome than a small one nobody can account for.

03

flightops

Rotation-cascade analysis over BTS on-time data
LiveReplicatedPython · React

An ontology-backed operational tool over US Bureau of Transportation Statistics data. Five typed objects, links between them, three recovery actions that return a diff instead of mutating anything, and a question-answering agent that can only reach the data through those same objects.

An aircraft flies six or seven legs a day, so a late inbound pushes the next departure, which pushes the one after. A controller looking at a board of late flights is looking at symptoms. The landing view therefore ranks roots by the minutes they force downstream, not flights by how late they are.

The premise was wrong, and the correction was better

The brief assumed delay amplifies down a rotation. Measured over a full month of BTS data it mostly does not: per-leg delay damps at 0.91×, and 44% of large delays propagate nothing at all because scheduled ground time absorbs them. Rather than leave that on one month, the whole measurement was re-run unchanged on a second, deliberately different month. It returned 0.91× again. That changed the product, not just the README: if half of large delays cost nothing downstream, the valuable thing is the ranking that tells you which half you are in.

The agent scores 9/10 against a SQL baseline's 8/10 on ten questions whose answers were computed by hand before the run. The scores matter less than the fact that the two fail in completely different places, both predicted in advance. Its one loss is the predicted one: a count of 106 objects through a tool that returns 40. Both baseline losses are citation failures, right answers that never named the object.
